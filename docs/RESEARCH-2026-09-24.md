# What we learned from other projects, 24 September 2026

You asked for two things: cutting-edge memory copied from projects that already do it well, and research in seven more areas. This page covers all of it in plain words. Each section ends with the ideas worth copying and whether anything waits on your decision.

Every research report was based on reading the other projects' actual code, not only their READMEs. Nothing could be measured on your PC from here. Every number a project reports about itself is **their claim**, and is marked that way. The full reports (with file and line references) were kept alongside the session work; this page is the summary.

Research read Epic-Jarvis at commit `5e3c91a`.

---

## 1. The short answer

- **Jarvis's safety design already beats every open-source project we looked at.** None of them has a review step before saving memory. Most of them let the model rewrite or delete memories on its own. The one scheduler that combines timers with tools (AnythingLLM) approves every tool call automatically. Jarvis refuses all of that on purpose, and the research found no reason to change it.
- **Research found several real bugs in Jarvis.** They are listed in section 2. The ones that do not need your decision are being fixed now.
- **The big gaps against ChatGPT, Gemini and Alexa are about time and control, not intelligence:**
  - timers, reminders and scheduled briefings;
  - a "temporary chat" that is not remembered;
  - showing which memories an answer used, with Forget on each one.
- **Voice can get noticeably faster with small changes in the two apps.** Today both apps wait for each sentence to finish playing before asking the PC for the next one. That leaves up to 3.5 seconds of silence inside an answer. The fix brings that to zero.
- **A newer model (Qwen 3.5 9B) looks like the right single model for your second graphics card.** It would handle long chats, pictures and learning in one model. Nothing changes until that card is installed and measured.

## 2. Bugs the research found

**Fixed without asking** (bugs, or text that says something untrue):

| # | What was wrong | Where |
|---|---|---|
| B1 | If the model writes a broken tool request, the details are silently replaced with nothing, so **you could get an approval card with an empty command on it**. | `backend/jarvis_agent.py`, `_one_call` |
| B2 | If Ollama cannot read the model's tool request at all, the whole answer ends with an error, with no second try. | `backend/jarvis_agent.py`, `one_round` |

**Wait on your decision** (section 9):

- **Automatic saving of sensitive topics.** When "Also remember sensitive topics automatically" is ON, a typed "Remember: my bank PIN is 4821" is saved without a card, in a file that is not encrypted.
  - Claude never auto-saves passwords, account numbers, ID numbers or immigration status, even with that kind of setting on.
- **What Forget removes.** Forget hides a fact from Jarvis, but the words stay in the memory file on your PC. They are not erased.
- **Planted notes.** After reading an email, Jarvis can still write into your Obsidian or Logseq notes without asking. You chose "no card" for those note tools earlier. The research shows an email could use that to plant text, or to copy a private file into a note that syncs to the cloud.
- **Hands-free voice.** When "Hey Jarvis" is on, a recording of your voice played near the microphone counts the same as you pressing the talk button. It can teach Jarvis facts, and memory answers are read aloud.

## 3. Memory

Five research runs studied 25 projects. They included mem0, Zep/Graphiti, Letta (MemGPT), LangMem, Memobase, MemoryOS, A-MEM, MIRIX, Cognee, LightRAG, HippoRAG, Generative Agents, supermemory and MemPalace, plus the benchmarks LongMemEval, LoCoMo and HaluMem.

### What Jarvis already does better

| | Jarvis | Most others |
|---|---|---|
| Review before saving | Yes: a card, or automatic only for your own live words | None |
| History kept, never deleted | Yes: every fact has two dates, one for "true from" and one for "known from" | They delete, overwrite, or cap at 100 memories |
| Defence against planted memories | Several layers (only your words, provenance, grounding, a sensitivity check) | None found in any of them |
| A memory-writing tool for the model | Excluded on purpose | Common, and it is how most published attacks work |

A 2026 paper (read only in summary) found that the one defence which worked against memory poisoning was **keeping outside text out of memory completely**. That is exactly what Jarvis does.

### Worth copying, best value first

1. **A memory test Jarvis can run on itself.** It would use about 60 made-up facts and 120 questions, and measure:
   - how often the right fact is found;
   - how often a wrong or replaced fact comes back;
   - what happens when the answer is "I don't know".

   A small version already ran here against the real memory code. Speed and size are fine: 10,000 facts searched in about 10 ms, in 18 MB. It also found a real weakness: **word search has no minimum match**, so a question like "what is my dog called?" can bring back "the owner's cat is called Biscuit". The test measures this first, then the fix gets tuned. (Copied from LongMemEval's scoring, MIT.)
2. **An "Always keep in mind" list.** A few facts you pin, copied word for word and never summarised, included in every answer. Today Jarvis only uses the 5 facts a search finds, so "owner is vegetarian" is missed when the question does not mention food. It is capped at about 1,200 characters. (Idea from Letta, MIRIX and MemoryOS; Jarvis's version never lets the model rewrite anything.)
3. **Knowing who "my sister" is.** A small table links names and aliases to facts ("my sister" means Priya), so "what's happening with my sister's wedding" finds the Priya facts. In a test here, today's search missed the answer and found someone else's wedding. With the table, the three right facts came first, in under 1 ms. (Idea and rules from Graphiti.)
4. **"What did I believe in June?"** Search can already filter by when a fact was true. It cannot yet filter by when Jarvis learned it, and chat never brings back an old, replaced fact. This adds both, with old facts labelled "(no longer true since …)".
5. **Real dates on facts.** "I moved to Leeds in January", said in March, is stored today as true from March. The learner would record the date you said it, and when the fact became true. (From Graphiti.)
6. **Counting repeats.** When you say something Jarvis already knows, it would note "said again on …" instead of ignoring it. That count shows which facts matter. (From ReMe and supermemory.)
7. **An overnight tidy that only raises cards.** Every project that tidies memory lets the model change things with nobody watching. Jarvis's tidy would:
   - find duplicates and contradictions;
   - ask "Is this still true?" about old, time-bound facts ("is working on …");
   - raise at most 5 cards a night, and change nothing by itself.
8. **A re-ranker.** A small model on the processor re-scores the top 20 search results. It closes gaps like "wedding" vs "getting married". It stays off until measured.

**Not copied:**
- anything that lets the model rewrite, merge or delete memories by itself;
- personality guessing (MemoryOS, Second-Me);
- any memory service that needs a server or an internet connection (mem0 and Graphiti both send usage data by default);
- summarising memories.

## 4. Safety: planted instructions

Planted instructions are hidden orders inside an email, file or web page that try to make the assistant do something you did not ask for.

- **Jarvis's strongest defence is already the right one:** nothing that leaves the PC runs without your approval card. The research confirmed this in code.
- **The gap:** reading tools and note-writing tools run without a card, and tool output reaches the model unmarked. A test with the real tool loop and a scripted "obedient" model showed an email getting two notes written with no card. The one card it did raise did not mention the email.
- **Measured here:** the research ran Jarvis's existing planted-instruction checks against 46 attack goals from AgentDojo, a public benchmark (MIT). They caught 33 of 46 in the most common attack style, with no false alarms on 383 ordinary emails and files. That is useful as a warning on a card, never as a block.
- **Being fixed now:** B3 and B4 from section 2, plus a test suite built from AgentDojo's attacks that runs without a model.
- **Not copied:** Google's CaMeL (an 8B model cannot write the programs it needs), Meta's SecAlign (needs data-centre graphics cards to train), NeMo Guardrails (heavy, and it detects the wrong thing), and mcp-scan (it sends your tool list to a company).

## 5. Safety: recordings and copied voices

- **Jarvis's voice check tells your voice from other people's. It cannot tell your voice from a recording of you, or from a good copy.** No local system can promise that. The best published detectors still miss 8-20% of fakes they have not seen.
- **Measured here:** three published fake-voice detectors, run on real voices and two modern computer voices:
  - two of them called about two thirds of real people fake;
  - the newest one called every computer voice real.

  None is safe to use as a gate.
- **What would actually help:**
  1. **Trust the talk button more than hands-free "Hey Jarvis"** for learning and for memory answers read aloud. Only hands-free can be triggered by a speaker playing near the microphone.
  2. **A "say these 3 words" check.** Words shown on your screen, never spoken, which a recording made earlier cannot contain.

  Both are your call (section 9).
- The voice-check wording in both apps and the docs will say plainly what it cannot do.

## 6. Models and tool reliability

- **Tool calls are not forced into a valid shape.** Ollama only reads the model's tool requests after it writes them. That causes bugs B1 and B2, which are being fixed. At most one quiet retry happens, before any card, so nothing runs without you.
- **The 16 tool descriptions take about 3,000 words' worth of the model's working memory.** The browser tool alone is about 850. Trimming them helps more than building a "which tools to show" router, which picked the right tool only 50-61% of the time in a test here.
- **For the second card: Qwen 3.5 9B** (Apache-2.0, March 2026).
  - It reads pictures, uses tools, and stores the conversation about 4.5 times more compactly than today's model.
  - On the 12 GB card it should hold 64,000 words' worth of conversation, perhaps 128,000. That is arithmetic, not a measurement.
  - It could replace today's plan of two models that take turns.
  - It does not fit the 8 GB card.
- **For the 8 GB card: Qwen 3.5 4B** is worth *testing*, not switching to. It would leave room for four to eight times more conversation. Nobody has measured whether it picks tools as well as today's model.
  - A measuring script is ready for your PC. It needs a download of about 3.4 GB, which is your call.

## 7. What the big assistants have that Jarvis lacks

| Feature | Fits Jarvis's rules? | Size |
|---|---|---|
| Timers, reminders, to-do list (answered instantly, without the model) | Yes | small-medium |
| Scheduled tasks and a morning briefing ("every weekday at 7") | Yes: setting one up is a card; each run may only read; anything that acts gets its own card | medium |
| Temporary chat (not remembered, not kept) | Yes | small |
| "Used in this answer" list with Forget on each fact | Yes (the PC already sends the fact ids) | small-medium |
| Web search and weather, one approved question at a time | Yes | medium |
| Calendar events and email drafts (saved as drafts, never sent) | Yes, one card each showing the full text | medium |
| Projects and "ask my documents" | Yes | medium-large |

**Not for Jarvis** (each breaks a rule):
- remote access through a company's relay or a chat app;
- public share links;
- memory stored in a company's cloud;
- schedules or "auto modes" that approve by themselves;
- speech-to-text on the phone;
- a Recall-style recording of everything on screen.

These are already on the list as tasks #52 and #53. The research ranks timers, reminders and scheduled briefings first, because every peer has them and Jarvis already has the pieces (the digest and the interruption budget). Nothing fires them yet.

## 8. Voice speed, and the first run

**Voice speed**
- Today, from the moment you stop talking to Jarvis's first word takes roughly 2.6-4 seconds. That is an estimate; the real figure is recorded on your PC.
- The slowest step is turning the first sentence into sound, about 1.3-1.6 seconds on the processor.
- **Being fixed now:** both apps will make the next sentence's sound while the current one plays. Measured here, the silence inside an answer went from 3.5 s to 0 s.
- **Your call (section 9):**
  - start speaking at the first comma: measured 2.0 s → 1.2 s to the first sound;
  - answer spoken questions in a shorter, spoken style.
- With the second card, the voice could move onto the graphics card. The target is about 1 second.

**First run.** A beginner gets stuck in 15 places. The worst are:
- typing a 43-character code from the PC into the phone;
- the phone and the desktop asking for two different forms of the PC's address;
- starting the backend by hand every time;
- the voice install being five long PowerShell lines.

The proposed fix is one "Set up Jarvis" checklist on both apps, where every fix button raises the normal card, plus pairing by QR code confirmed by a card on the PC. That is task #73.
